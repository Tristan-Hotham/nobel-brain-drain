The Nobel brain drain

Where Nobel laureates were born, against where they were working when they won. {{abroad.n|int}} prizes in physics, chemistry, medicine and economics, 1901 to {{source.y1|raw}}, on one map: the countries that grow laureates, the countries that collect them, and the one that has collected more than all the rest together.

A Nobel prize is awarded to a person, but it is also claimed by places: by the town where the laureate was born, the country on their first passport, and the university that employed them on the day the telephone rang from Stockholm. These are often not the same. Of the {{abroad.n|int}} prizes in physics, chemistry, physiology or medicine and economics for which the Nobel Prize's own data give both a birthplace and an affiliation at the time of the award, {{abroad.abroad|int}} ({{abroad.pct|pc}}) went to someone working in a country other than the one they were born in.

Draw a line from each birthplace to each workplace and the map below appears: a thick red rope from Europe to the north-east of the United States and California, finer threads from Japan, China, India and South Africa, and a blue web inside Europe that thins out after 1933. Use the prize buttons and the decade slider to filter it, switch to women or men, search for a laureate, a country or an institution, and click any line for the person behind it.

    02 · Gain and lossOne country gained, almost everyone else lost

    For every country, count the prizes won by people working there and subtract the prizes won by people born there. The United States comes out {{us.net|int}} ahead: {{us.n|int}} prizes won by people working in America, against {{us.born|int}} won by Americans by birth, wherever they worked. Only {{countries.n_gain|int}} countries finish in credit at all, and after the United States the gains are small: Switzerland +{{countries.che.net|int}}, the United Kingdom +{{countries.gbr.net|int}}, Denmark and Sweden +1 each.

    Net gain or loss of laureates, by country

    Prizes won by people working in a country minus prizes won by people born there; present-day borders. Physics, chemistry, medicine and economics. Click a bar to see the country on the map.

    The losers are a roll-call of twentieth-century upheaval. Poland's {{countries.pol.net|int}} looks like the biggest loss, but {{countries.pol_de_then|int}} of its {{countries.pol.born|int}} laureates were born in what was then Germany, in Breslau and the Prussian east, and {{countries.pol_de_then_to_de|int}} of them won in Germany: a story about borders moving rather than people. Hungary sent {{germany.hungary.abroad|int}} of its {{germany.hungary.n|int}} laureates abroad, Austria {{germany.austria.abroad|int}} of {{germany.austria.n|int}}. Canada ({{countries.can.net|int}}) lost scientists to the United States next door: all {{countries.can.left|int}} Canadian-born laureates who won abroad were working there; China ({{countries.chn.net|int}}) and India ({{countries.ind.net|int}}) lost almost all the laureates they grew.

    03 · The great importerThe United States

    Until the 1930s the United States was a minor Nobel nation: {{us.pre1940_share|pc}} of the science prizes awarded before 1940 went to people working there. Germany led in each of the first four decades. From the 1940s the United States has led every decade, and since 1950 it has taken {{us.since1950_share|pc}} of all prizes in the four subjects.

    Share of prizes won by people working in the United States

    By award decade, physics, chemistry, medicine and economics. Hover for numbers.

    A large part of that American haul was born somewhere else. Of the {{us.n|int}} prizes won in the United States, {{us.abroad|int}} ({{us.pct|pc}}) went to people born abroad, from {{us.n_orig|int}} countries. The largest suppliers are the United Kingdom ({{us.orig.0.1|int}}), Canada ({{us.orig.1.1|int}}), Germany ({{us.orig.2.1|int}}), France ({{us.orig.3.1|int}}) and Japan ({{us.orig.4.1|int}}). In the 2010s and 2020s the foreign-born share of American prizes was {{us.d2010.us_pct|pc}} and {{us.d2020.us_pct|pc}}: higher than in any decade since the 1950s. Of every prize won by someone working outside their birth country, {{us.share_imports|pc}} were won in America.

    04 · GermanyThe loss that arrived late

    Germany dominated the early Nobel prizes. Up to 1932, {{germany.work_to1932|int}} of the {{germany.n_to1932|int}} science prizes ({{germany.work_share_to1932|pc}}) went to people working in Germany, and almost every German-born laureate of that time stayed: only {{germany.early_abroad_pct|pc}} of German-born winners up to 1932 were working abroad. Since 1933, Germany's share has been {{germany.work_share_since1933|pc}}, and {{germany.late_abroad_pct|pc}} of German-born winners have been working in another country.

    Germany before and after 1933

    Left: share of each decade's prizes won by people working in Germany. Right: laureates born in the German lands of the day (as the Nobel data name the birth country at the time, including places now in Poland, Russia and France), by where they won.

    The prizes lag the history. The Nazi dismissals of 1933 pushed hundreds of scientists out of German universities, many of them Jewish, but their Nobel prizes came years or decades later, in Britain and America: Otto Stern in {{germany.named.Otto Stern.y|raw}}, Hans Krebs and Max Born in the 1950s, Hans Bethe in {{germany.named.Hans Bethe.y|raw}}. Among German-born laureates born between 1880 and 1929, {{germany.exiles_n|int}} won while working abroad, {{germany.exiles_us|int}} of them in the United States and {{germany.exiles_gbr|int}} in Britain. The data cannot say when or why each of them left (the list below also includes people who moved as children, or after the war), only where they were when the prize came.

    05 · JapanThe late riser that stays at home

    Japan had {{japan.before2000|int}} science laureates in the whole of the twentieth century and {{japan.since2000|int}} since 2000. Since 2000 it has been the third most common birth country of science and economics laureates, behind only the United States and the United Kingdom, with {{japan.since2000_share|f1}}% of all prizes, against {{japan.pre2000_share|f1}}% before. Unlike most other countries outside the United States, Japan keeps its laureates: most won at home, at Kyoto, Nagoya and Tokyo universities and at companies such as Shimadzu and Asahi Kasei. The exceptions went to America: {{japan.n_abroad|int}} of {{japan.n|int}}, including Yoichiro Nambu in Chicago, Shuji Nakamura at Santa Barbara and Syukuro Manabe at Princeton.

    Japanese-born laureates, by award decade

    Physics, chemistry, medicine and economics, by where they were working when they won.

    One caveat shows how slippery "where the work was done" can be. Hideki Yukawa, Japan's first laureate in {{japan.first|raw}}, worked out his meson theory in Japan in the 1930s, but the Nobel data list Columbia University first among his affiliations in 1949, so he counts here as a prize won in the United States. Affiliation at the time of the award is a good guide to where a laureate's career had taken them, and a rougher one to where the winning work was done.

    06 · InstitutionsThe top of the table is American

    Counting every affiliation the Nobel data list at the time of an award, {{institutions.top.0.name|raw}} comes first with {{institutions.top.0.n|int}} prizes, then {{institutions.top.1.name|raw}} ({{institutions.top.1.n|int}}), {{institutions.top.2.name|raw}} ({{institutions.top.2.n|int}}) and {{institutions.top.3.name|raw}} ({{institutions.top.3.n|int}}). {{institutions.top10_us|int}} of the top ten are American; the University of Cambridge ({{institutions.top.9.n|int}}) is the highest outside the United States. Grouped together, the Max Planck institutes and their predecessors the Kaiser Wilhelm institutes would hold {{institutions.mpg|int}}.

    07 · MobilityBorn abroad, decade by decade

    The share of prizes going to people working outside their birth country has been remarkably steady: {{abroad.min_dec.pct|pc}} in the {{abroad.min_dec.dec|raw}}s at its lowest, {{abroad.max_dec.pct|pc}} in the {{abroad.max_dec.dec|raw}}s at its highest, and {{abroad.d2020.pct|pc}} in the 2020s so far. What changed is not how much laureates moved but where they went: before 1933 mostly between European countries, afterwards overwhelmingly to the United States.

    08 · WomenFrom {{women.before1960_pct|f1}}% to {{women.d2020.pct|pc}}

    Women have won {{women.n|int}} of the {{source.n_recs|int}} Nobel prizes awarded to people. Before 1960 it was {{women.before1960|int}} of {{women.before1960_n|int}}; in the 2020s so far it is {{women.d2020.w|int}} of {{women.d2020.n|int}} ({{women.d2020.pct|pc}}). The sciences have moved more slowly: women won {{women.sci_before2000_pct|f1}}% of science and economics prizes before 2000 and {{women.sci_since2000_pct|f1}}% since. Physics has had {{women.by_cat.0.w|int}} women laureates in {{women.by_cat.0.n|int}} prizes and economics {{women.by_cat.3.w|int}} in {{women.by_cat.3.n|int}}.

    Women laureates in the sciences were about as likely as men to be working abroad: {{women.flow_w_abroad_pct|pc}} of {{women.flow_w_n|int}}, against {{women.flow_m_abroad_pct|pc}} of men. Marie Curie, born in Warsaw and working in Paris, is one of the earliest lines on the map; the Nobel data give her no affiliation for her 1903 physics prize, so only her 1911 chemistry prize is drawn.

    09 · Peace and LiteratureWriters and peacemakers mostly stay put

    The Nobel data give no affiliation for Peace and Literature laureates, so they are not in the analysis above. Most do have a country of residence at the award: {{peace_lit.placed|int}} of {{peace_lit.n|int}}. By that measure {{peace_lit.pct|pc}} were living outside their birth country, {{peace_lit.lit|pc}} of writers and {{peace_lit.pea|pc}} of peace laureates, far fewer than the scientists. Switch on Literature and Peace in the map above to see them as dashed lines.

    Since 1950, {{us.since1950_share|pc}} of Nobel prizes in the sciences and economics have gone to people working in the United States, and a third of those to people born somewhere else.

    10 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Prizes and people. The official Nobel Prize API lists {{source.n_api|int}} laureates, {{source.n_org|int}} of them organisations. I counted one record per prize awarded to a person, {{source.n_recs|int}} in all, so the {{source.n_multi_prize|int}} people with two prizes (Marie Curie, John Bardeen, Frederick Sanger, Linus Pauling and Barry Sharpless) count twice. Names, prizes, years, sex, birthplaces and affiliations come from the API. The flow analysis covers the {{source.n_sci|int}} prizes in physics, chemistry, medicine and economics, less {{source.n_sci_noaff|int}} with no affiliation in the API (among them Marie Curie in 1903 and Friedrich von Hayek and Gunnar Myrdal in 1974) and {{source.n_sci_nobirth|int}} with no birthplace: {{abroad.n|int}} prizes. Where the API lists several affiliations ({{source.n_multi_aff|int}} prizes), I used the first that has a place; only {{source.n_multi_cty|int}} list affiliations in more than one country.

    Matching and placing. I matched the API to a Wikidata query of physics, chemistry and medicine laureates by Wikidata ID, checking that prize and year agree: all {{source.n_sci3|int}} such prizes matched, {{source.n_match_id|int}} by ID and the rest by name, prize and year (two laureates have duplicate Wikidata items). The Wikidata extract also contains a fictional laureate, Sheldon Cooper, who matched nothing. Birthplaces are placed at Wikidata's coordinates where available ({{source.n_b_wd|int}} prizes), otherwise at the API's present-day city point ({{source.n_b_api|int}}), a Natural Earth town ({{source.n_b_ne|int}}) or the country's centre ({{source.n_b_country|int}}). Where both exist they are a median {{source.bw_km_median|f1}} km apart. Workplaces are placed at a Wikidata employer of the same person whose name matches the affiliation and lies within 60 km of the API's city ({{source.n_w_emp|int}} of the {{abroad.n|int}}), else at the API's city point, a city centroid ({{source.n_w_api|int}}), or a hand-set point ({{source.n_w_manual|int}}). Every row of the data has a method column.

    Countries. Birth and work countries are the present-day countries the API gives ("countryNow"), so a laureate born in Breslau in 1868 counts as Polish-born. The Germany section uses the country named at the time of birth instead.

    What this is not. Affiliation at the award is not the same as where the prize-winning work was done, which can be decades earlier and elsewhere. Birth country is not nationality: many laureates born abroad moved as children. And Nobel prizes are a tiny, idiosyncratic sample of science, decided by committees whose choices have their own geography and their own biases, not least against women.
